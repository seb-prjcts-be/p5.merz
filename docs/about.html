About p5.merz

When I was in school, we took the bus to Paris, where I saw work by loads of painters and sculptors I already adored. There was one question I held on to until we were on the bus back to Ghent, when I finally asked my teacher:
“Who is KS?”
She smiled and said:
“That is Kurt Schwitters.”

The name p5.merz comes out of that affection for Schwitters' work. His typographical work reached a high point in the magazine Merz (1923–1932). Twenty-one issues were published, numbered 1–24. Issues 10, 22 and 23 were never published.

Merz itself is a found piece. Schwitters cut the syllable out of an advertisement for the Commerz- und Privatbank and glued it into a collage in 1919; from then on the word named everything he made. Merz 2 (April 1923) is “Nummer i”, and i was his shortest rule: take a printed piece as you find it, the choice is the work. That is what fundsachen() does with 28 pieces from the covers. Merz 8/9, “Nasci” (April–July 1924), was made with El Lissitzky; the cover itself says the typography is his, so the blue rules and the red NASCI in the catalogue are Lissitzky’s. The last issue, Merz 24 (1932), printed the Ursonate, set by Jan Tschichold; the top of this site sets its first word, Fümms, from the printer’s case. Schwitters also drew a systematic alphabet of his own, the Systemschrift of 1927. The geometric letters here are not a copy of it. They are our own construction on a five-unit grid, made so that a bowl or a stem can be taken out and used alone, but it is good company to be in.

The German function names (satz, bauteile, schnitt, riss, andruck, stempel, fundsachen) are a nod to the vocabulary Kurt Schwitters used for his work, much like the cut-up terms in p5.gysin. The goal is not to copy historical pages, but to have a small set of building blocks that leaves room for your own sketches.
